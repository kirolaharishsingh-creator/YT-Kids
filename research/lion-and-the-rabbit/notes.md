# Research: The Lion and the Rabbit

## Source

One of the best-known tales from the **Panchatantra**, the ancient Sanskrit
collection of animal fables attributed to Vishnu Sharma (compiled roughly
between the 3rd century BCE and 3rd century CE). This story appears across
many Panchatantra editions and retellings; exact book/chapter placement varies
by edition/translator, so before publishing, cross-check the specific
retelling below against at least one direct English translation of the
Panchatantra (e.g. Arthur W. Ryder's translation, public domain) rather than
relying only on secondary retellings. **Not yet verified against a primary
translation — flagged for fact-check before this script is treated as final.**

## The story (standard retelling)

A powerful, arrogant lion terrorizes a forest, killing an animal a day. The
animals strike a deal: they'll send him one animal daily as tribute if he
stops hunting them down himself. One day it's the rabbit's turn. The rabbit,
clever and calm, arrives deliberately late. The furious lion demands to know
why. The rabbit says he was delayed by another lion in the forest who
threatened him — and offers to show the lion this rival. He leads the lion to
a deep well and tells him to look in. The lion sees his own reflection,
mistakes it for a rival, roars at it, and leaps in to attack — and drowns.
The forest is freed.

## Moral

**Wit and courage beat brute strength.** (Sometimes phrased: "cleverness
defeats tyranny.")

## Why this fable for the pilot

- Only two characters on screen (lion, rabbit) + one prop location (the well)
  — minimal cast for a first AI-generation test of the style guide.
- Clear three-beat structure that maps directly onto a 15–20 second format:
  setup (tribute deal) → twist (rabbit's trick) → payoff (lion falls for it).
- Well-known enough that the audience recognizes the shape of the story fast,
  which matters when you only have ~15 seconds to land it.
- No violence needs to be shown on-screen — the lion's fall can be implied
  (splash/silence) rather than depicted, keeping it clearly family-friendly.
