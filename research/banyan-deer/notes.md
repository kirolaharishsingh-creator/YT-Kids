# Research: The Banyan Deer's Offer

## Source

From the **Jataka Tales** (Nigrodhamiga Jataka / "The Banyan Deer," #12) —
part of the Pali Buddhist canon's birth-story collection, widely retold in
Indian and South/Southeast Asian children's story collections as a general
moral fable, independent of religious framing.

✅ **Fact-checked 2026-09-16.** Cross-referenced via web search against
multiple independent sources converging on the same facts: a scholarly Pali
translation (ancient-buddhist-texts.net, Ja 12), wisdomlib.org, and
literature-study summaries (kalawati175.com.np, bookreview.com.np). Core
facts confirmed:
- The two herd leaders are named **Nigrodha** ("Banyan," the Bodhisatta/
  golden deer of the title) and **Sākha** ("Branch") — both golden-hued,
  each leading 500 deer
- The king is **Brahmadatta** of Benares (Varanasi)
- The agreement was alternating days between the two herds, one deer
  offered each time
- The pregnant doe was from **Sākha's** herd; Sākha refused her request to
  wait; she then appealed to Nigrodha, who took her turn himself
- **Correction to the original draft below:** the king's mercy extended to
  **all animals**, not just deer — he ended hunting in his kingdom
  entirely, not only the deer-tribute arrangement. Worth reflecting in the
  script's moral beat if there's room.

## The story (standard retelling)

Two herds of deer lived near a king's forest — one led by the golden Banyan
Deer, the other by the Branch Deer. To stop the king's hunters from
panic-chasing and killing many deer at once, the herds struck a deal: one
deer would be sent to the king's kitchen each day, the herds taking turns.
One day, the lot fell on a pregnant doe from the Branch Deer's herd. She
begged her leader to let another take her place until her fawn was born —
he refused, saying the lot was hers and he would not ask anyone else to die
for her. Desperate, she went to the Banyan Deer instead. Unwilling to ask
any other deer to take her place either, the Banyan Deer chose to go to the
chopping block himself, in her stead. When the king saw the noblest deer in
the forest offering his own life for a stranger's unborn fawn, he was so
moved that he not only spared the Banyan Deer, but ended the daily-tribute
arrangement entirely and granted the deer sanctuary from hunting altogether.

## Moral

**A true leader gives before he takes — mercy answered with mercy.**
(Sometimes phrased: "compassion moves even those in power.")

## Why this fable for video #2

- Natural follow-up to "The Lion and the Rabbit": both are Panchatantra/Jataka
  classics about power and cleverness, but this one shifts the theme to
  **self-sacrifice and mercy** rather than wit — good variety early in the
  series.
- Clear three-beat shape: the arrangement (setup) → the doe's plea and the
  Banyan Deer's choice (turn) → the king's mercy (payoff) — maps cleanly to
  the 15–20 second format already proven on the pilot.
- Compact cast for the visuals: the Banyan Deer, the pregnant doe, and the
  king — no crowd scenes required beyond a suggested herd in the background.
