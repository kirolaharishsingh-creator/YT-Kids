# Content Backlog — 100 Fables (20-second narration drafts)

Each entry is a **narration-script draft** — the spoken voiceover for one 20-second
video — not yet a full shot-by-shot production script. Before any of these go
into production, follow the same steps fable #1 did:

1. Fact-check the story against a primary/reputable source (see confidence tags below)
2. Break the approved narration into visual beats in `video-projects/<slug>/`
3. Build the shot list against `character-bible/style-guide.md`

**Confidence key:**
- ✅ **High** — well-documented, widely retold story; safe to move to research/fact-check next.
- ⚠️ **Verify** — general shape is right but specific plot details vary by
  retelling/edition and are less certain here; cross-check carefully before
  treating any detail as fact.

Numbering is a production-order suggestion, not a strict schedule — reorder
freely in `video-projects/`.

## Panchatantra (1–25)

**001 — The Lion and the Rabbit** ✅ *(already researched/scripted — see `scripts/lion-and-the-rabbit.md`)*
> Every animal owed the tyrant lion a daily meal. When it was the rabbit's turn, he arrived late, claiming a rival lion threatened him, and led the beast to a well. The lion saw his own reflection, roared at his "rival," and leapt in. Moral: wit beats strength.

**002 — The Monkey and the Crocodile** ✅
> A monkey and a crocodile were friends, until the crocodile's wife demanded the monkey's heart as a delicacy. The crocodile offered him a ride home to "fetch" it — the monkey realized the trap mid-river and claimed he'd left his heart on his tree. Moral: quick thinking can save your life.

**003 — The Blue Jackal** ✅ *(fact-checked and scripted — see `scripts/blue-jackal.md`)*
> A jackal fell into a vat of blue dye and, seeing his strange new color, convinced the forest he was a king sent to rule them. He lived like royalty — until a chorus of jackals howling at night made him forget himself and howl back, exposing him. Moral: a borrowed disguise never survives your true nature.

**004 — The Talkative Tortoise** ✅ *(fact-checked and scripted — see `scripts/talkative-tortoise.md`)*
> Two geese offered to fly their tortoise friend to a new pond by having him grip a stick in his mouth while they carried the ends. He was warned not to speak mid-flight — but when children below mocked him, he opened his mouth to answer back, and fell. Moral: loose lips undo you.

**005 — The Musical Donkey** ✅
> A donkey working nights with a jackal insisted on singing despite every warning that his braying would wake the farmers. He sang anyway, was caught, and beaten. Moral: vanity invites danger.

**006 — The Merchant's Iron Scales** ✅
> A merchant lent his iron scales to a friend, who later claimed mice had eaten them. When the friend's son "vanished," the merchant said an eagle carried him off — mirroring the absurd excuse right back. Moral: a lie always has a hole in it.

**007 — The Three Fish** ✅
> Three fish lived in a pond a fisherman planned to net. The wise one left at the first warning; the clever one escaped by playing dead during the catch; the fatalist one did nothing and was caught. Moral: foresight beats fatalism.

**008 — The Loyal Mongoose** ✅
> A mongoose guarded a Brahmin's baby and killed a cobra that attacked the crib, but returned covered in blood. The Brahmin's wife, assuming the worst, killed the mongoose instantly — only to find the baby safe and the dead snake beside it. Moral: don't act in anger before you know the truth.

**009 — The Crane and the Crab** ✅
> An aging crane pretended to offer fish safe passage to a better pond, secretly eating each one on the way. A wise crab caught on and, agreeing to be carried, gripped the crane's neck and killed it first. Moral: cleverness can outwit even a trusted friend's trick.

**010 — The Foolish Lion's Clever Jackal** ✅
> A short-tempered lion kept a jackal as his advisor, who learned to phrase every warning so the lion never felt insulted — keeping both of them alive through the lion's worst moods. Moral: a wise servant can manage even a dangerous master.

**011 — The Mouse Turned Princess** ✅
> A sage turned a mouse into a girl and raised her as his daughter, offering her the sun, the cloud, the wind, and the mountain as husbands — each admitting someone stronger existed. In the end, she chose a mouse. Moral: nature always shows through in the end.

**012 — The Brahmins Who Revived the Lion** ✅
> Four scholarly Brahmins found lion bones; three used their learning to reassemble and bring it to life, ignoring the fourth's warning. The revived lion killed them instantly. Moral: knowledge without wisdom is dangerous.

**013 — The Mice and the Elephants** ✅
> A herd of elephants trampled a mouse colony fleeing drought. When hunters later trapped the elephants in nets, the mice — remembering the elephants had once let them shelter nearby — gnawed through the ropes to free them. Moral: kindness returned is never wasted.

**014 — The Crows and the Serpent** ✅
> A serpent kept eating a crow couple's chicks. Unable to fight it directly, the crows stole a queen's gold necklace and dropped it into the serpent's hole — guards followed the trail and killed the snake while retrieving it. Moral: the weak can defeat the strong with cunning.

**015 — The Ass in the Tiger Skin** ✅
> A washerman dressed his donkey in a tiger skin to graze freely in others' fields without being chased off. It worked, until the donkey heard another donkey braying and joined in — revealing himself and getting caught. Moral: a disguise fails the moment you act like yourself.

**016 — The Farmer and the Snake** ✅
> A snake killed a farmer's son; the farmer struck back and nearly killed the snake. Both offered peace, but neither could forget what happened, and the snake finally left rather than live in constant suspicion. Moral: broken trust can never fully heal.

**017 — The Foolish Friend and the Fly** ✅
> A bear kept watch over his sleeping friend and, trying to kill a fly that landed on the man's face, crushed his skull with a rock instead. Moral: a foolish friend is more dangerous than a wise enemy.

**018 — The Jackal and the War-Drum** ⚠️ *(also appears in Hitopadesha — verify which detail belongs to which version)*
> A jackal, terrified by a booming sound in the forest, worked up his courage to investigate and found only an old war-drum banging in the wind. Confidence restored, he tore it open looking for food — and found nothing but noise. Moral: don't let fear, or overconfidence, replace investigation.

**019 — The Monkey and the Wedge** ✅
> A monkey watched carpenters leave a log half-split with a wedge holding it open, and curiously sat where the gap was to pull the wedge free — the log snapped shut on him instantly. Moral: meddling in what you don't understand backfires.

**020 — The Weaver Who Wanted Wings** ⚠️
> A weaver, convinced by a clever carpenter friend of an impossible flying-machine scheme to win a princess, disguised himself as a god to enter the palace — and was exposed the moment he was asked to actually perform a miracle. Moral: greed makes you reach for what was never yours.

**021 — The Heron and the Fish** ⚠️ *(near-duplicate of #009 in some editions — verify distinct version before producing both)*
> A heron, too old to hunt fish directly, told them a fisherman was coming and offered to carry them one by one to a safer pond — quietly eating each one along the way, until a crab exposed the trick. Moral: every trick eventually meets a sharper one.

**022 — The Old Tiger's False Piety** ✅
> A tiger, too old and slow to hunt, sat by a lake pretending to be a pious ascetic offering gold bracelets to any traveler who'd come close enough to take one. A wary traveler saw through the act and refused to step near the water. Moral: kindness offered by a predator is still a trap.

**023 — The Owl and the Crows** ⚠️
> Owls and crows, once bitter enemies, went to war after a failed peace summit. Outnumbered, the crows' king sent a spy disguised as a wounded owl-hater into the owls' own nest — learning their weakness and burning their tree hollow at dawn. Moral: know who you let close before trusting them.

**024 — The Camel in the Lion's Court** ⚠️
> A lion's jackal, crow, and wolf courtiers, starving and unable to hunt the lion's trusting camel friend directly, each pretended to nobly "offer themselves" as food first — knowing the lion would refuse each — until the camel, moved by their display, offered himself and was killed. Moral: loyalty is not protection from those who mean you harm.

**025 — The Washerman's Jackal** ⚠️
> A jackal watched a washerman's donkey get praised simply for carrying loads without complaint, and tried to imitate the donkey's plain behavior to win the same treatment — only to be beaten for acting strangely out of character. Moral: imitation without understanding invites ruin.

## Hitopadesha (26–33)

**026 — The Brahmin's Broken Pot** ✅
> A poor Brahmin, given a pot of rice, lay daydreaming about selling it, buying goats, then cows, then marrying rich and scolding his future wife for ignoring him — and swung his arm mid-daydream, shattering the pot. Moral: daydreams built on nothing shatter easily.

**027 — The Four Friends and the Hunter** ✅
> A mouse, crow, tortoise, and deer were close friends. When a hunter's net trapped the deer, the mouse gnawed him free, the crow warned of the hunter's return, and the slow tortoise — caught while helping — was saved when the deer lured the hunter away. Moral: true friends rescue each other in turn.

**028 — The Old Merchant's Young Wife** ⚠️
> An aging merchant married a much younger woman who grew resentful of the mismatch; a parable told at court used their unhappiness to warn against choices made for appearance rather than compatibility. Moral: a mismatch invites its own trouble.

**029 — The Lion King's Foolish Advisors** ⚠️
> A lion king trusted two flattering jackals over his more cautious, honest ministers, and nearly led his kingdom into a war neither side wanted. Moral: bad counsel, warmly delivered, is still bad counsel.

**030 — The Frog King's Dangerous Alliance** ⚠️
> A frog king, feuding with his own kind, invited a black snake into the pond as an ally — who then began quietly eating the frogs one by one, including the king's own family. Moral: a dangerous ally is still dangerous.

**031 — The Traveler Saved from the Well** ✅
> A kind-hearted traveler pulled a tiger, a monkey, a snake, and a man out of a dry well. The animals warned him the man would repay kindness with betrayal — and later, that very man reported him falsely to a king for a reward. Moral: kindness isn't always returned in kind — but is still worth giving.

**032 — The Lion King's Sleepy Rule** ⚠️
> A lion king grew lazy and let his ministers run wild, ignoring small injustices until the kingdom's smaller animals began fleeing the forest entirely. Moral: a ruler who looks away lets small dangers grow into large ones.

**033 — The Fish Who Ignored the Warning** ✅ *(shares its shape with #007 — this is the Hitopadesha telling)*
> Among three fish in a pond a fisherman eyed, one left immediately at the first sign of danger, one stayed and outsmarted the net when it came, and one refused to believe the danger was real at all — and was caught. Moral: foresight beats denial.

## Jataka Tales (34–53)

**034 — The Golden Goose** ✅
> A goose that could lay one golden feather a day was kept by a poor family who grew impatient and plucked all his feathers at once, hoping for a fortune — the feathers turned to ordinary ones and never grew back golden again. Moral: greed loses you everything, a little at a time.

**035 — The Monkey King's Bridge** ✅
> When hunters cornered a troop of monkeys by a river, their king stretched his own body across the gap as a living bridge so his troop could escape over him, absorbing the hunters' arrows himself. Moral: a true leader sacrifices himself first.

**036 — The Banyan Deer's Offer** ✅
> Two deer kings agreed to send one deer daily to a king's table so the herd wouldn't be hunted in panic. When a pregnant doe's turn came, the Banyan Deer offered himself in her place — moving the king to end the whole practice. Moral: mercy asked for should be mercy given.

**037 — The Hare's Gift to the Moon** ✅
> A hare, a monkey, a jackal, and an otter each prepared to feed a hungry beggar (actually a disguised god testing them). The hare, having nothing to offer, leapt into the fire to give his own body as food — and was placed on the moon in tribute. Moral: true generosity holds nothing back.

**038 — The Timid Rabbit and the Falling Sky** ✅
> A rabbit, startled by a falling coconut, panicked and convinced the entire forest the sky was collapsing — until a wise lion traced the sound back to its harmless source and calmed the stampede. Moral: don't spread fear before you check the facts.

**039 — The Jackal Judge and the Otters' Fish** ✅
> Two otters caught a large fish together but couldn't agree how to split it, so they asked a passing jackal to divide it fairly — who awarded himself the fattest middle piece as his "fee" and left them the scraps. Moral: a false judge always keeps the biggest share.

**040 — The Brave Little Quail** ✅
> A baby quail, too young to fly, was threatened by a rampaging elephant destroying the forest. Using only clever appeals to other animals working together, the quail orchestrated the elephant's downfall despite being the smallest creature involved. Moral: courage and cleverness protect even the smallest.

**041 — The Elephant Who Mourned His Friend** ⚠️
> A royal elephant refused to eat or work after his close friend, a small dog, was taken away by a stranger — until the king, realizing the bond, had the dog returned. Moral: true friendship outlasts anything.

**042 — The Turtle, the Deer, and the Woodpecker** ⚠️
> Three animal friends worked together to outsmart a hunter closing in on one of them — each using their own particular skill at the right moment to save the group. Moral: friends who act together outwit any hunter.

**043 — The Boastful Crow** ⚠️
> A crow, jealous of a swan's graceful swimming, tried to imitate it and nearly drowned, becoming a running joke among the other birds. Moral: those who copy without skill only embarrass themselves.

**044 — The Patient Buffalo** ⚠️
> A mischievous monkey repeatedly provoked a calm buffalo grazing nearby, expecting anger — but the buffalo's patience eventually so bored the monkey that he gave up entirely. Moral: patience wins where anger fails.

**045 — The Minister's Test of Friendship** ⚠️
> A wise minister tested which of the king's "friends" would still stand by him if he lost everything — and found only one who had never asked for anything in return. Moral: true friends stay through hardship, not just plenty.

**046 — The King Who Learned to Listen** ⚠️
> A proud king dismissed every advisor who brought him bad news, until a crisis he'd been warned about repeatedly finally struck unchecked. Moral: a ruler who won't hear the truth invites disaster.

**047 — The Merchant's Honest Servant** ⚠️
> A servant, given the chance to keep gold his master had forgotten about, returned it in full — and was rewarded years later when the merchant remembered exactly who he could trust. Moral: honesty is remembered long after the moment passes.

**048 — The Parrot and the Burning Tree** ✅
> A parrot who had lived his whole life in one great tree refused to abandon it even as it caught fire, instead flying back and forth trying to douse the flames with water from his wings — moving even the rain god to save the tree. Moral: loyalty to home is its own reward.

**049 — The Two Stubborn Goats** ⚠️
> Two goats met on a narrow bridge, each refusing to back up and let the other cross first, until both fell into the river below. Moral: stubbornness helps no one cross safely.

**050 — The Crab and the Crane** ✅ *(Jataka telling of the same shape as #009 — verify which version to keep)*
> A crane that had been secretly eating fish it offered to "rescue" met its match in a crab, who let itself be carried only long enough to strike first. Moral: betrayal eventually meets betrayal.

**051 — The Jackal's Borrowed Roar** ⚠️
> A jackal, having slipped into a lion's den and worn a piece of its mane, strutted through the forest expecting the same fear a real lion commanded — until an actual lion's roar sent him running. Moral: imitation of power isn't the same as having it.

**052 — The Flattered Deer** ⚠️
> A deer who enjoyed a crow's constant flattery ignored a tortoise friend's warnings about a nearby trap, and was caught exactly where the crow — who profited from the deer's carcass — had been quietly steering him. Moral: flattery is bait, not friendship.

**053 — The Patient Physician Bird** ⚠️
> A small bird carefully and slowly removed a bone stuck in a crocodile's throat, refusing to rush despite the danger, and lived to tell of it precisely because it didn't panic. Moral: patience and the right method matter more than speed.

## Akbar–Birbal (54–70)

**054 — Birbal's Khichdi (The Cold Lake Bet)** ✅
> A poor man claimed he could stand in a freezing lake all night for a reward, and did — but when asked how he endured it, admitted he'd stared at a distant lamp for comfort. Birbal ruled that "warmth" from a lamp so far away didn't count, exposing the technicality. Moral: a clever question exposes an empty boast.

**055 — Whose Fault Is It?** ⚠️
> Akbar posed a dispute designed to trap Birbal into blaming either the king or a commoner — Birbal's answer cleverly satisfied both sides without taking either one down. Moral: fairness means looking past who's loudest.

**056 — The Weight of the Elephant** ✅
> Asked to weigh a royal elephant without any scale large enough, Birbal floated it in a boat, marked the waterline, then reloaded the boat with stones until it sank to the same mark — weighing the stones instead. Moral: the simplest solution is often hiding in plain sight.

**057 — Milk and Water** ⚠️
> A dishonest milkman was caught diluting his milk with water when Birbal devised a simple public test that separated the two in front of the whole court. Moral: a lie in the mix always separates eventually.

**058 — Twelve Fingers, One Truth** ⚠️
> Challenged with a riddle designed to make any answer seem foolish, Birbal's reply won laughter and admiration instead of embarrassment. Moral: wit answers what force cannot.

**059 — Birbal and the Ants** ✅
> Asked exactly how many ants lived in the kingdom, Birbal confidently gave a specific number — and when challenged, replied that if it was wrong, it was only because some had wandered into a neighboring kingdom that day. Moral: some questions are answered best with humor, not exact numbers.

**060 — The Astrologer's Own Prediction** ⚠️
> A court astrologer predicted the exact day of Birbal's death to unsettle him. Birbal instead asked the astrologer to predict his OWN death date — and when the man refused, pointed out he couldn't even see his own fate clearly, let alone anyone else's. Moral: turn a trick back on the trickster.

**061 — The Barber's Test** ⚠️
> Birbal, disguised, tested which of Akbar's courtiers would still show him respect with no crown in sight — and found the palace barber to be the only one whose kindness didn't depend on rank. Moral: true loyalty shows only when there's nothing to gain.

**062 — The Cleverest Thief** ⚠️
> To catch a thief among several suspects, Birbal gave each an identical stick and claimed the guilty one's stick would grow overnight — the thief, trying to hide the "growth," trimmed his stick shorter and gave himself away. Moral: a guilty conscience always gives itself away.

**063 — The King's Impossible Portrait** ⚠️
> Akbar asked for a portrait that made him look neither too old nor too flattering — an impossible brief every painter failed, until Birbal's solution satisfied the king by reframing the request entirely. Moral: a clever answer can satisfy even an impossible demand.

**064 — Birbal's Bag of Wisdom** ⚠️
> Asked to demonstrate his wisdom in a single object, Birbal produced something small and ordinary and explained its lesson so well the whole court agreed no treasure could outweigh it. Moral: real wisdom travels lighter than it looks.

**065 — The Foolish Question Contest** ⚠️
> Rival courtiers tried to embarrass Birbal with a string of trick questions meant to make him look foolish in front of the king — each answer instead made them look worse. Moral: a wise man never needs to mock to prove his worth.

**066 — The Servant Who Outsmarted the Court** ⚠️
> A lowly palace servant solved a puzzle that stumped every minister, and Birbal made sure the credit — and reward — went to him rather than being claimed by someone of higher rank. Moral: cleverness has no rank.

**067 — The King's New Advisor** ⚠️
> A smooth-talking newcomer to court nearly replaced Birbal in Akbar's favor through pure flattery, until his advice led to a costly mistake Birbal had quietly warned against. Moral: flattery fools a king faster than an enemy can.

**068 — Birbal Finds the Real Culprit** ⚠️
> When a valuable item went missing and panic swept the court, Birbal calmly waited and watched rather than accusing anyone outright — and the true thief's own nervous behavior gave them away within a day. Moral: patience uncovers what panic hides.

**069 — The Two Pots of Gold** ⚠️
> Two men each claimed ownership of a pot of gold found on the road; Birbal's simple test — asking each what they'd do with it — revealed which one was lying about ever owning it. Moral: greed always reveals its own hand.

**070 — The Village That Couldn't Lie** ⚠️
> Birbal proposed a single rule for a dispute-ridden village that made lying so immediately costly that honesty became the easiest option — solving in one decree what years of punishment hadn't. Moral: one honest rule can outsmart a hundred schemes.

## Tenali Raman (71–85)

**071 — Tenali and the Thieves' Bath** ✅
> Thieves cornered Tenali Raman at night demanding his valuables. He calmly suggested they all bathe in the river first "for good luck" before dividing the loot — then quietly slipped away with the thieves' own clothes while they were still in the water. Moral: quick thinking beats brute numbers.

**072 — The King's Insomnia Cure** ⚠️
> When King Krishnadevaraya couldn't sleep, every remedy the court physicians tried failed — until Tenali's unconventional, almost absurd solution worked precisely because it was so simple no one else had thought to try it. Moral: sometimes the cleverest cure is the simplest one.

**073 — The Poet's Challenge** ⚠️
> A visiting poet challenged the court to complete a difficult verse on the spot; Tenali's improvised answer won not through preparation but pure wit. Moral: true wit needs no rehearsal.

**074 — The Trader's False Weights** ⚠️
> A merchant cheating customers with rigged weights was exposed when Tenali arranged a public demonstration that made the deception impossible to hide. Moral: a cheat's own trick can be turned against him.

**075 — The Parrot's Judgment** ⚠️
> To settle a dispute nobody could resolve honestly, Tenali staged a clever trick involving a "talking" parrot that revealed the truth better than any direct questioning could. Moral: clever staging can reveal a hidden truth.

**076 — The Dancer's Riddle** ⚠️
> A visiting performer tried to embarrass the court with an impossible riddle disguised as a dance challenge; Tenali's answer, delivered with total confidence, won the exchange. Moral: confidence backed by wit beats confidence alone.

**077 — The Four Deaf Men** ✅
> Four men, each hard of hearing in a different way, had a conversation where none of them actually heard the others' questions correctly — leading to a chain of comically wrong answers Tenali later used to illustrate a point at court. Moral: miscommunication multiplies trouble, not truth.

**078 — The Rival Poet's Undoing** ⚠️
> A boastful poet arrived at court claiming to outclass Tenali; a single exchange of verses was enough to send him home humbled. Moral: arrogance is easy to outshine.

**079 — The Barber's Secret** ⚠️
> A minor palace barber turned out to know a piece of court gossip that solved a mystery baffling everyone above his rank — and Tenali made sure his insight was heard. Moral: even the smallest servant can hold the biggest key.

**080 — The Mango Tree Dispute** ⚠️
> Two neighbors argued bitterly over which of them owned the fruit of a tree growing exactly on their shared property line, until Tenali's ruling satisfied both without dividing the tree itself. Moral: cleverness settles what greed cannot.

**081 — Tenali's Disguise at Court** ⚠️
> Tenali disguised himself to teach an arrogant nobleman a lesson about how differently he treated people based on appearance alone — revealing himself only once the lesson had landed. Moral: the right disguise can teach the wrong person a lesson.

**082 — The King's Test of Loyalty** ⚠️
> When Krishnadevaraya secretly tested which courtiers would defend him even when he seemed to be in the wrong, only Tenali's response proved to be loyalty rather than flattery. Moral: true devotion doesn't need an audience.

**083 — The Beggar's Wisdom** ⚠️
> A beggar at the palace gates answered a philosophical question that stumped the court's most educated scholars, and Tenali made sure his wisdom, not his rags, was what people remembered. Moral: humility can outwit pride every time.

**084 — Tenali and the Vain Painter** ⚠️
> A court painter who only ever flattered his subjects in portraits was gently exposed by Tenali, who arranged for the painter's own flattering self-portrait to be compared against reality. Moral: flattery paints a prettier picture than truth, but it never lasts.

**085 — The Salt in the Story** ⚠️
> A feast prepared for the king came out bland and forgettable until Tenali pointed out — using a simple parable about salt — that the smallest missing ingredient can ruin an otherwise perfect effort. Moral: sometimes the smallest ingredient changes everything.

## Vikram–Betaal / Baital Pachisi (86–93) ⚠️ Verify all before production

*The Baital Pachisi is a frame story of 25 riddle-tales told by a vetala (spirit) to King Vikramaditya, each ending in a moral riddle. Specific plot details vary significantly by translation/retelling — treat every entry below as a rough shape only, to be confirmed against a specific translation before scripting.*

**086 — The Riddle of the Three Suitors** ⚠️
> A princess's hand is claimed by three men, each having helped save her in a different way — the vetala challenges King Vikramaditya to judge which one truly deserves her. Moral: true worth isn't always the obvious choice.

**087 — Who Is the Real Father?** ⚠️
> A child is raised by two different men, each with a claim to fatherhood through different circumstances — the vetala's riddle asks the king to decide whose claim matters more. Moral: the law of the heart isn't always the law of blood.

**088 — The Brahmin's Wish for Wisdom** ⚠️
> A Brahmin is granted a powerful boon he doesn't fully understand the cost of, and the consequences ripple out to those around him. Moral: some gifts cost more than they're worth.

**089 — The Loyal Wife's Test** ⚠️
> A wife's devotion is tested under extraordinary circumstances that force her to prove loyalty in a way no one expected to need proof of. Moral: loyalty proven under pressure is loyalty proven true.

**090 — The King's Unanswerable Question** ⚠️
> The vetala poses a riddle seemingly without a fair answer — and King Vikramaditya's response reframes the entire question rather than answering it directly. Moral: every riddle has an answer if you keep your wits.

**091 — The Two Brothers' Bargain** ⚠️
> Two brothers strike a hasty deal to divide something valuable between them, only to find the terms benefit one far more than the other once the details play out. Moral: a bargain struck in haste is rarely struck fairly.

**092 — The Magician's Trade** ⚠️
> A magician uses trickery to take something rightfully belonging to someone else, and the vetala's tale follows how that stolen power or position eventually slips away again. Moral: power taken by trickery is never kept for long.

**093 — The Silent Princess** ⚠️
> A princess who has taken a vow of silence must find a way to communicate something urgent without breaking it — her solution becomes the heart of the riddle. Moral: sometimes the bravest answer is the one nobody expects.

## Singhasan Battisi (94–97) ⚠️ Verify all before production

*32 tales told by apsara-statues guarding King Vikramaditya's throne, each illustrating one of his virtues. Specific episodes vary by retelling — verify against a specific source before scripting.*

**094 — The Beggar Who Tested a King's Generosity** ⚠️
> A beggar arrives at Vikramaditya's court with an impossible request, testing whether the king's famous generosity has limits. Moral: true generosity gives without keeping count.

**095 — The King's Unbreakable Word** ⚠️
> Vikramaditya makes a promise that turns out to cost him far more than expected to keep — and keeps it anyway. Moral: a promise kept at great cost is a promise worth making.

**096 — The Judge Who Would Not Bend** ⚠️
> Vikramaditya, acting as judge in a dispute involving someone close to him, rules strictly by fairness rather than favor. Moral: justice bends for no one, not even a king.

**097 — The Guardian Statue's Warning** ⚠️
> Each time a later king tries to sit on Vikramaditya's legendary throne, one of the 32 statues tells a story explaining why he isn't yet worthy of it. Moral: even a great throne must be earned, not just claimed.

## Gopal Bhar (98–100) ⚠️ Verify all before production

*Bengali folk tales of the court jester Gopal Bhar in the court of King Krishnachandra. Widely retold with regional variation — verify specifics before scripting.*

**098 — Gopal Bhar's Royal Cure** ⚠️
> The king falls into a mood no physician can cure, until Gopal Bhar's unconventional, humor-driven approach succeeds where medicine failed. Moral: laughter can heal what medicine cannot.

**099 — Gopal Bhar Outwits the Priest** ⚠️
> A pompous priest tries to use his position to take advantage of a villager, and Gopal Bhar's clever intervention exposes the abuse of authority. Moral: cleverness cuts through false authority.

**100 — The Jester's Honest Answer** ⚠️
> Asked a question at court that no one else dares answer honestly for fear of the king's reaction, Gopal Bhar's blunt, funny truth-telling is what actually gets heard. Moral: the fool in the room is sometimes the wisest one.
